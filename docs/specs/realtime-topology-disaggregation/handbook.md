# 0. Purpose and Architectural Direction

This document defines the core algorithms, data structures, inference mechanisms, and runtime behavior of the Load Disaggregation System.

The objective is not simply to associate electrical devices with CT Readers.

The objective is to construct a continuously updated model of the electrical installation that can:

1. Understand which physical loads are observable through which Readers.
2. Infer measurement relationships between Readers.
3. Detect electrical load changes in real time.
4. Correlate measurement changes with known devices and unknown loads.
5. Estimate device power consumption using multiple sources of evidence.
6. Distinguish individual devices when they are mathematically identifiable.
7. Preserve uncertainty when they are not identifiable.
8. Continuously update device-to-Reader relationships as evidence accumulates.
9. Detect changes in the physical installation and measurement topology.
10. Improve its models without corrupting previously collected evidence.

The architecture must be designed around one fundamental distinction:

**A Reader measures electrical activity. A device model explains electrical activity. The inference engine determines how much of the observed activity can be attributed to each device.**

These are three different responsibilities.

The system must not confuse:

- A Reader measurement with a device power estimate.
- A statistical correlation with a physical circuit relationship.
- A device state change with an actual electrical change.
- An unexplained residual with random measurement noise.
- A plausible device allocation with an identifiable device allocation.

The system must support incomplete observability as a normal operating condition.

It must also support multiple valid explanations of the same measurements until enough evidence becomes available to distinguish them.

## 0.1 Recommended Core Architecture

The system should contain the following cooperating engines:

1. Measurement Ingestion and Normalization.
2. Measurement Topology Engine.
3. Residual and Unknown Load Engine.
4. Device Power Model Engine.
5. Dynamic Event Matching Engine.
6. Joint Inference Engine.
7. Device-Reader Mapping Matrix.
8. Confidence and Identifiability Engine.
9. Online Learning and Reconciliation Engine.
10. Historical Evidence and Versioning System.

The Mapping Matrix is a central, continuously updated representation shared by several engines. It must not be treated as the entire system's source of truth.

# 1. Foundational Data Model

Before designing the inference algorithm, define what the system is actually modeling.

## 1.1 Four Different Types of Objects

Do not represent every electrical object as a generic Device.

The system must distinguish:

### A. Physical Device

A real-world appliance or load.

Examples:

- Television.
- Refrigerator.
- HVAC.
- Dishwasher.
- Lighting installation.
- Network equipment.
- Electric dryer.
- Smart Plug.

A Physical Device may or may not be connected to Home Assistant.

It may be directly monitored, indirectly monitored, or not individually monitored at all.

### B. Load Component

A physically or electrically distinguishable component of a device.

Examples:

- HVAC compressor.
- HVAC fan.
- HVAC auxiliary heating element.
- Refrigerator compressor.
- Refrigerator defrost heater.
- Blower motor.
- Individual lighting channel.

This distinction is necessary because one logical device may contain multiple independently varying electrical loads.

A single HA Entity may control multiple Load Components.

Multiple HA Entities may also control different aspects of one Physical Device.

### C. HA Entity

A Home Assistant entity or control interface.

Examples:

- `light.living_room`.
- `climate.main_floor`.
- `switch.tv`.
- `cover.living_room_blinds`.

An HA Entity provides behavioral evidence. It is not automatically equivalent to a physical load.

One Physical Device can have multiple HA Entities.

One HA Entity can affect multiple Load Components.

Some Physical Devices have no HA Entity.

### D. Measurement Endpoint

A source of electrical measurements.

Examples:

- Main Panel CT Reader.
- Subpanel CT Reader.
- Branch Circuit CT Reader.
- Smart Plug Energy Monitor.
- Dedicated appliance energy monitor.

Each Measurement Endpoint must have an independent identity and configuration history.

A Measurement Endpoint must describe what it measures, not what the application assumes it measures.

## 1.2 Entity Relationships

The relationships are not necessarily one-to-one.

Examples:

- One device → multiple Load Components.
- One device → multiple HA Entities.
- One device → multiple Measurement Endpoints.
- One Reader → many Physical Devices.
- Multiple Readers → the same Physical Device.
- One HA Entity → multiple physical load changes.
- One Measurement Endpoint → a load that is not represented in HA.

Do not force these relationships into a single Device-to-Reader foreign key.

# 2. Measurement Model

The entire system depends on a correct measurement model.

## 2.1 Reader Measurement Definition

Every Reader must expose a normalized measurement record.

At minimum:

```text
ReaderMeasurement
  reader_id
  timestamp
  received_at
  active_power_w
  current_rms_a
  voltage_rms_v
  reactive_power_var
  power_factor
  energy_wh
  phase
  leg
  voltage_reference
  measurement_type
  measurement_window_ms
  calibration_version
  quality_flags
```

Fields not supported by the actual Reader may be null.

Do not fabricate measurement fields.

The system must know whether a value represents:

- Instantaneous measurement.
- Window-averaged measurement.
- Device-reported power.
- Calculated power.
- Accumulated energy.
- A measurement from one electrical leg.
- A measurement from multiple electrical legs.

## 2.2 Measurement Compatibility

Do not directly compare all measurements just because they contain numbers.

Active Power in W should be preferred for compatible power comparisons.

Current in A must retain its phase and electrical reference.

Current from different phases or voltage systems cannot automatically be treated as additive.

The system must explicitly account for:

- Split-phase systems.
- L1 and L2.
- 120V loads.
- 240V loads.
- Paired-leg measurements.
- CT polarity.
- Calibration differences.
- Reverse power flow.
- Solar and battery systems.

A measurement compatibility layer must determine which Reader observations can participate in a given inference equation.

## 2.3 Signed Measurements

Power changes must be signed.

For an event:

\[
\Delta Y_r=Y_{r,\text{after}}-Y_{r,\text{before}}
\]

Positive values represent an increase in measured power.

Negative values represent a decrease.

Never apply absolute value to the primary event data.

Absolute values may be used in carefully defined statistics, but they must not replace signed measurements in inference.

# 3. The Mathematical Foundation

## 3.1 Measurement-to-Device Model

Let:

- \(r\) represent a Reader.
- \(i\) represent a Physical Device or Load Component.
- \(Y_r(t)\) represent the measurement from Reader \(r\).
- \(P_i(t)\) represent the physical power of load \(i\).
- \(H_{ri}(t)\) represent the measurement relationship between Reader \(r\) and load \(i\).
- \(U_r(t)\) represent unexplained load within the measurement scope.
- \(\epsilon_r(t)\) represent measurement noise.

The fundamental model is:

\[
Y_r(t)=
\sum_i H_{ri}(t)P_i(t)
+U_r(t)
+\epsilon_r(t)
\]

This equation is the foundation for both real-time inference and historical learning.

The observation operator \(H\) is derived primarily from Measurement Topology and device-to-reader observability.

It must not automatically be a simple binary matrix.

Depending on the electrical system and measurement type, it may represent:

- Full observation.
- Partial observation.
- Calibrated measurement scaling.
- Paired-leg observation.
- Shared-load observation.
- Unknown or uncertain observation.

A value of 1 can indicate that a load contributes fully to a compatible Reader measurement.

A value of 0 indicates that the load is not observed by that Reader.

A calibrated coefficient may be used when the measurement physics justifies it.

Do not use arbitrary coefficients simply to improve regression fit.

## 3.2 Why Use Both Absolute and Delta Models?

The absolute model is:

\[
Y_r(t)=\sum_iH_{ri}P_i(t)+U_r(t)+\epsilon_r(t)
\]

The delta model is:

\[
\Delta Y_r=
\sum_iH_{ri}\Delta P_i
+\Delta U_r+\Delta\epsilon_r
\]

These models serve different purposes.

### Absolute model

Useful for:

- Current power estimation.
- Standby estimation.
- Baseline modeling.
- Reader energy reconciliation.
- Absolute power constraints.

### Delta model

Useful for:

- Detecting device activation.
- Matching HA events to Reader events.
- Identifying Reader containment relationships.
- Learning power changes.
- Detecting unknown load activation.
- Reducing the effect of stable background consumption.

The delta model is often more informative for event-based attribution because unchanged background loads partially cancel.

However, delta inference does not replace the absolute model.

For example, a refrigerator consuming 80W continuously may create no new delta event, but its absolute power is still important for energy accounting.

## 3.3 Residual-Aware Measurement Equation

For a Parent Reader and a set of compatible Child Readers:

\[
Y_p(t)=
\sum_c\beta_{pc}Y_c(t)
+R_p(t)
+\epsilon_p(t)
\]

Here:

- \(Y_p\) is the parent measurement.
- \(Y_c\) represents child measurements.
- \(\beta_{pc}\) represents an appropriate measurement relationship.
- \(R_p(t)\) represents unexplained consumption.
- \(\epsilon_p(t)\) represents measurement noise.

The corresponding event equation is:

\[
\Delta Y_p=
\sum_c\beta_{pc}\Delta Y_c
+\Delta R_p
+\Delta\epsilon_p
\]

**A Parent Reader is not required to be fully explained by its Child Readers.**

The Child Readers can provide high-quality evidence even when the Parent Reader contains substantial unexplained load.

An unexplained Parent change is not sufficient evidence to reject an existing containment relationship.

# 4. Measurement Topology

## 4.1 Topology Must Be Inferred Before Device Attribution

The Topology Engine establishes the available measurement structure.

The Disaggregation Engine uses this structure as prior information.

Do not attempt device attribution as though every Reader were an independent meter.

For example:

```text
Main CT
  ├── Subpanel CT
  │     ├── Circuit CT A
  │     └── Circuit CT B
  └── Circuit CT C
```

If a device contributes 100W to Circuit A:

- Circuit CT A may observe +100W.
- Subpanel CT may observe +100W.
- Main CT may observe +100W.

These are three observations of the same physical consumption.

They are not three separate 100W loads.

## 4.2 Typed Measurement Relationships

Use a typed directed graph.

Required relationships:

### `CONTAINS`

Reader A observes a measurement scope containing Reader B's scope.

Only validated containment relationships participate in hierarchy calculations.

### `SHARED_LOAD`

Two Readers observe the same physical load through different measurement endpoints.

### `PAIRED_LEG`

Two Readers observe electrically related legs of the same multi-leg load.

### `CORRELATED`

Two Readers have repeatable measurement correlation but physical containment has not been established.

A correlation edge must not be treated as a containment edge.

## 4.3 Asymmetric Topology Evidence

Child-to-Parent evidence is usually more informative than Parent-to-Child evidence.

When a Child changes by 100W and the Parent repeatedly changes by the corresponding amount, this supports the hypothesis that the Parent contains that Child.

When the Parent changes by 100W but no known Child changes, this does not disprove the relationship.

The additional consumption may be unmonitored load.

Topology inference must use:

- Signed delta agreement.
- Absolute measurement constraints.
- Repeated event evidence.
- Phase and voltage compatibility.
- Reader quality.
- Residual behavior.
- Conflicting evidence.
- Time consistency.

A single event must not immediately make an edge structural.

Topology follows the evidence in both directions (ADR-0043). An edge's state
is recomputed from *recent* evidence at every settle: decaying support and
conflict weights (one event never moves an edge past CANDIDATE) let it move up
and down. "Structural" means only that the observation operator H uses the
edge right now, not a terminal confirmation. Decay is by verdict count, not
wall clock, so quiet periods do not erode a true edge and replay stays
deterministic. Parent-only changes are neutral. A pin is the only freeze.

## 4.4 Topology as a Versioned Prior

The topology at time \(t\) is:

\[
T(t)
\]

The observation operator becomes:

\[
H(t)=H(T(t),M(t))
\]

where \(M(t)\) represents device-reader mappings.

A topology change may modify the observation operator without changing a Physical Device Power Model.

For example, when a Smart Plug moves from Circuit A to Circuit B:

- The Plug's own measured power may remain unchanged.
- The Plug's device power model may remain valid.
- Its Reader relationship to Circuit A changes.
- Its Reader relationship to Circuit B changes.
- The mapping and topology versions must be updated.

Old evidence must remain associated with its historical topology version.

# 5. Real-Time Inference Architecture

Real-time inference should not be implemented as one large function that attempts to recalculate every device from every Reader sample.

It should use a staged, event-driven architecture.

The system has three computational loops.

## 5.1 Fast Measurement Loop

Typical responsibility:

- Receive measurements.
- Normalize values.
- Update rolling buffers.
- Detect significant changes.
- Maintain baseline and noise estimates.
- Maintain current measurement quality.

This loop should not perform expensive historical regression after every sample.

## 5.2 Event Inference Loop

Triggered by:

- A Reader power change.
- An HA state change.
- A Smart Plug event.
- A residual change.
- A possible topology change.

Responsibilities:

- Build an event group.
- Find possible device candidates.
- Match events across Readers.
- Run joint inference.
- Assess identifiability.
- Update the current Matrix snapshot.
- Publish provisional or confirmed estimates.

## 5.3 Background Learning Loop

Runs asynchronously or in controlled batches.

Responsibilities:

- Update long-term power models.
- Reconcile event associations.
- Refit topology hypotheses.
- Update residual distributions.
- Recompute mapping confidence.
- Perform historical replay.
- Create new model versions.

The fast path must remain responsive even when historical learning is running.

# 6. Real-Time Processing Pipeline

The following is the canonical event processing sequence.

```text
Reader / HA / Smart Plug Input
            |
            v
1. Measurement Normalization
            |
            v
2. Timestamp Alignment
            |
            v
3. Rolling State and Noise Update
            |
            v
4. Change Detection
            |
            v
5. Event Window Collection
            |
            v
6. Event Group Formation
            |
            v
7. Dynamic Candidate Matching
            |
            v
8. Joint Inference
            |
            v
9. Identifiability and Confidence
            |
            v
10. Matrix Snapshot Update
            |
            v
11. Provisional / Confirmed Result
            |
            v
12. Controlled Model Learning
```

Each stage must produce structured data that can be inspected and replayed.

## 6.1 Stage 1 — Measurement Normalization

Convert raw measurements into the canonical Reader measurement format.

Validate:

- Units.
- Timestamps.
- Measurement type.
- Phase and leg.
- Calibration.
- Plausible physical ranges.
- Quality flags.

Invalid measurements must not silently enter the inference engine.

## 6.2 Stage 2 — Timestamp Alignment

Reader timestamps may not be perfectly synchronized.

Preserve at least:

- `observed_at`
- `received_at`
- `measurement_window_ms`

Normalize events into a common time domain.

Use configurable alignment windows.

Do not require exact timestamp equality.

For window-averaged measurements, account for the measurement window rather than treating the sample timestamp as an exact physical transition time.

Late-arriving events must be supported.

When historical data arrives late, reconcile the relevant event group without rewriting unrelated results.

## 6.3 Stage 3 — Rolling Baseline and Noise

Maintain independent rolling statistics for every Reader.

Examples:

- Baseline power.
- Median power.
- Median absolute deviation.
- Standard deviation where appropriate.
- Expected short-term noise.
- Recent event frequency.
- Measurement quality.

A robust noise estimate may use:

\[
\sigma_r\approx1.4826\cdot MAD_r
\]

This estimate is suitable for approximately symmetric noise around a stable median. The implementation must account for non-Gaussian and changing measurement distributions where observed.

Use a noise-aware event score:

\[
z_r=
\frac{Y_r-\widehat{Y}_{r,\text{baseline}}}
{\max(\sigma_r,\sigma_{\min})}
\]

Combine statistical significance with absolute thresholds and hysteresis.

Do not use one global watt threshold for every Reader.

A 10W change may be meaningful for one Reader and irrelevant for another.

Baseline adaptation must avoid absorbing a persistent step change immediately.

After a suspected load change, distinguish a new operating level from a transient noise excursion.

## 6.4 Stage 4 — Change Detection

Use suitable methods such as:

- Noise-aware thresholding.
- Hysteresis.
- CUSUM.
- EWMA.
- Robust change-point detection.

Detection must identify:

- Positive transitions.
- Negative transitions.
- Sustained changes.
- Short transients.
- Repeated periodic behavior.
- Missing or unreliable measurements.

The detection system should assign an event quality score.

Do not force every minor fluctuation to become an event.

## 6.5 Stage 5 — Event Window Collection

When a candidate event is detected, preserve a window around it.

For example:

- Pre-event data: 30 seconds.
- Post-event data: 120 seconds.

These are initial configurable values, not fixed requirements.

Different loads require different windows.

An HVAC start, a lighting change, and a motorized blind movement may have very different response profiles.

Store:

- Reader measurements.
- Signed deltas.
- Baseline estimates.
- Measurement noise.
- Data quality.
- Event start and end.
- Timestamp uncertainty.
- Topology version.

Use a circular buffer so useful pre-event measurements are available without permanently storing every high-resolution sample.

## 6.6 Stage 6 — Event Group Formation

A logical event may generate multiple observations.

For example:

- HA device command.
- Device state update.
- Smart Plug power change.
- Circuit Reader change.
- Subpanel Reader change.
- Main Reader change.

These observations should be grouped when they plausibly represent one physical event or coordinated set of events.

Possible grouping evidence:

- Temporal proximity.
- Shared HA context ID.
- Parent and child HA context.
- Automation execution.
- Reader correlation.
- Device response profile.
- Known device dependencies.

HA context identifiers are useful evidence, but not absolute proof that two physical changes occurred at the same time.

An Event Group should contain multiple sub-events with individual timestamps.

Do not collapse all measurements into one timestamp.

Simultaneous device changes must remain distinguishable as separate candidate causes inside one Event Group.

# 7. Dynamic Matching Mechanism

This is one of the most important parts of the system.

Dynamic Matching determines which devices and Load Components are plausible causes of the observed electrical changes.

It must not rely on permanent one-to-one associations.

A device can be observed by multiple Readers.

A Reader can observe many devices.

A device may change its behavior over time.

A Reader may observe a load that is not currently represented in HA.

Therefore, matching is an ongoing probabilistic and evidence-driven process.

## 7.1 Candidate Generation

When an Event Group is created, generate a candidate set.

Candidates can come from:

1. HA state changes occurring within the relevant time window.
2. HA attribute changes.
3. Devices with learned power response profiles.
4. Devices previously associated with affected Readers.
5. Smart Plug measurements.
6. Unknown Load Profiles.
7. Devices whose topology visibility includes the affected Readers.
8. Devices with predicted delayed responses.
9. Previously identified device groups.

Do not limit candidates to devices that changed HA state.

A refrigerator compressor may start without a corresponding HA event.

An unintegrated appliance may produce a completely legitimate electrical event.

## 7.2 Topology-Based Candidate Filtering

Use the current topology to determine where each candidate could be observed.

For each device \(d\), determine its predicted Reader observation signature:

\[
\mathbf{h}_d=
[H_{1d},H_{2d},\ldots,H_{Rd}]
\]

This signature describes which Readers are expected to observe the device.

Use topology to reject physically incompatible explanations.

However, do not use uncertain topology edges as hard constraints.

Candidate filtering must preserve alternatives when topology is provisional.

## 7.3 Temporal Matching

Each device model must describe its expected response timing.

For an instantaneous light:

- Response delay is small.
- Expected power change is close to the state transition.

For a motorized blind:

- There may be a startup transient.
- Power may remain elevated during motion.
- Power may return to standby after completion.

For HVAC:

- The thermostat state can change before the compressor starts.
- Fan and compressor may activate at different times.
- The load can have distinct startup and steady-state behavior.

Represent the expected response using a device-specific temporal response profile.

Conceptually:

\[
\widehat{\Delta P_i(t)}
=
h_i(\tau,\text{transition},\text{attributes})
\]

where \(h_i\) is the expected response as a function of elapsed time, transition type, and relevant device attributes.

For devices with delayed or transient responses, the model may be represented as a response kernel.

Candidate matching should compare the entire observed event window against this expected response, not only the first detected delta.

## 7.4 Matching Evidence

For every candidate device, calculate matching evidence using:

### A. Temporal agreement

Does the observed Reader event occur within the expected response window?

### B. Sign agreement

Does the device transition predict the observed direction?

For example:

- ON should usually increase consumption.
- OFF should usually decrease consumption.
- Brightness increase may increase power.
- A motor finishing movement may reduce power.

### C. Amplitude agreement

Is the observed power change consistent with the device's known power profile or uncertainty range?

### D. Reader signature agreement

Do the affected Readers match the expected measurement visibility?

A device directly measured by a Smart Plug should have a strong direct observation at that Plug.

A device within a branch circuit should normally affect compatible upstream measurements.

### E. Topology consistency

Does the candidate explanation agree with the current measurement topology?

### F. Model agreement

Does the observed response resemble previously learned device behavior?

### G. Measurement quality

Are the relevant Reader observations reliable?

### H. Context evidence

Does HA event lineage or automation context support the association?

These signals should contribute to a structured matching score or likelihood.

A conceptual form is:

\[
\log P(E|d)=
w_tL_t+
w_sL_s+
w_aL_a+
w_vL_v+
w_TL_T+
w_mL_m+
w_qL_q
\]

This formula is a design abstraction.

The production implementation must calibrate weights or use a statistically appropriate probabilistic model.

Do not assume the terms are independent when they are derived from the same underlying measurements.

## 7.5 Unknown Load Is Always a Competing Candidate

Every matching process must retain an unknown-load alternative.

For example:

A Reader increases by 120W.

The possible explanations may be:

- Known Device A started.
- Known Device B started.
- An unknown device started.
- Multiple devices changed simultaneously.
- A known device changed its internal operating state.
- A measurement artifact occurred.

Do not force the best known device candidate to win when the evidence is weak.

The unknown candidate is necessary to prevent false device attribution.

## 7.6 Matching Is Not a One-Time Assignment

A match evolves through evidence accumulation.

Recommended states:

- `UNMATCHED`
- `PROPOSED`
- `PROVISIONAL`
- `CONFIRMED`
- `AMBIGUOUS`
- `CONFLICTED`
- `REJECTED`
- `EXPIRED`

For example:

The first 100W transition may produce a provisional match.

Five repeated transitions with similar timing and Reader signatures may support confirmation.

A later contradictory event may reduce confidence or create a conflict.

The historical association must remain traceable even if a newer model changes the conclusion.

## 7.7 Simultaneous Event Matching

Do not process every device candidate independently and greedily assign the strongest one.

When several devices change simultaneously, evaluate the joint explanation.

For example:

```text
Event Group 42

HA:
  Living Room Light: OFF -> ON
  Kitchen Light: OFF -> ON
  TV: STANDBY -> ACTIVE

Reader observations:
  Circuit A: +45W
  Circuit B: +60W
  Main: +110W
```

The system should evaluate all candidate combinations against the multi-Reader observation vector.

One device may explain part of the change.

Several devices may explain different parts.

Some observed power may remain unexplained.

The matching engine must pass the full candidate set to Joint Inference instead of committing to independent guesses.

# 8. Joint Inference Engine

Dynamic Matching generates plausible explanations.

Joint Inference determines which combinations are consistent with the measurements and available models.

## 8.1 Joint Measurement Equation

For an event group \(k\):

\[
\Delta\mathbf{Y}_k=
H_k\Delta\mathbf{P}_k+
\Delta\mathbf{U}_k+
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k
\]

Where:

- \(\Delta\mathbf{Y}_k\) is the multi-Reader observation vector.
- \(H_k\) is the measurement operator derived from topology and mappings.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{P}_k\) is the set of candidate device power changes.
- \(\Delta\mathbf{U}_k\) is unexplained load change.
- \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k\) is measurement error.

For delayed devices, the event model must include the expected temporal response.

For simultaneous devices, the solution must jointly evaluate their combined effects.

## 8.2 Objective Function

A suitable conceptual optimization is:

\[
\min_{\Delta P,\Delta U}
\sum_r
w_r\rho\left(
\frac{
\Delta Y_r-\sum_i H_{ri}\Delta P_i-\Delta U_r
}{
\sigma_r
}
\right)
+\lambda_mL_{\text{model}}
+\lambda_uL_{\text{unknown}}
+\lambda_tL_{\text{temporal}}
\]

The terms represent:

- Measurement residual loss.
- Deviation from known device power models.
- Plausibility of unknown load changes.
- Temporal response consistency.

Use robust loss functions when appropriate.

Use physical constraints only when valid.

For ordinary consuming loads, non-negative absolute power may be a reasonable constraint.

For signed deltas, negative values are legitimate.

For solar and battery systems, reverse power flow must be explicitly supported.

Do not simply force all inferred deltas to be positive.

The unknown-load penalty must be based on learned residual behavior.

If the residual penalty is too high, the system will falsely assign unexplained consumption to known devices.

If it is too low, the system will place too much consumption into residual.

This balance must be measurable and configurable.

## 8.3 Measurement Covariance and Double Counting

Nested Reader measurements are correlated.

For example, a Parent and Child may both observe the same physical load.

They must not be treated as statistically independent evidence.

When combining observations, consider their covariance:

\[
\Sigma=
\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})
\]

Use covariance-aware fitting where sufficient evidence exists.

When covariance is unavailable, adopt conservative weighting instead of assuming independence.

A direct Smart Plug measurement can be a particularly useful anchor because it measures an individual load rather than only a larger aggregate scope.

## 8.4 Identifiability

A mathematically good fit does not imply that individual devices were identified.

Consider:

\[
\Delta Y= \Delta P_A+\Delta P_B
\]

If A and B always change together, the measurements may establish:

\[
\Delta P_A+\Delta P_B=250W
\]

But they do not establish:

\[
\Delta P_A=125W
\]

and:

\[
\Delta P_B=125W
\]

That equal split is an arbitrary allocation.

The system must assess identifiability using appropriate methods, including:

- Feature matrix rank.
- Column correlation.
- Condition number.
- Singular value analysis.
- Posterior uncertainty.
- Measurement diversity.
- Independent event history.
- Direct measurement anchors.

The correct solution may be a Device Group rather than individual Device estimates.

Recommended identifiability states:

- `IDENTIFIABLE`
- `PARTIALLY_IDENTIFIABLE`
- `GROUP_IDENTIFIABLE`
- `NON_IDENTIFIABLE`
- `INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE`

### Mandatory output behavior

When a group has a known aggregate power of 250W:

- Group estimate: 250W.
- Individual device estimates: null unless separately identifiable.
- Individual bounds: only mathematically justified bounds.
- Individual identifiability: NON_IDENTIFIABLE.
- Evidence references: available.
- Source Readers: listed.

When physical assumptions establish non-negative individual power and no tighter constraints are available, [0W, 250W] may be a valid bound for each member.

Never present this interval as a precise measurement.

## 8.5 Estimate Status

Every result must distinguish:

- `MEASURED`
- `ESTIMATED`
- `ALLOCATED`
- `UNKNOWN`

Also distinguish:

- Current point estimate.
- Valid lower and upper bounds.
- Measurement source.
- Inference source.
- Confidence.
- Identifiability.

An allocated group estimate must not be presented as an individually measured device value.

# 9. The Device-Reader Mapping Matrix

The Matrix is a central concept of this architecture.

It must provide an understandable, continuously updated representation of the relationship between Physical Devices and Measurement Endpoints.

The Matrix is not merely a static lookup table.

It is a structured, time-dependent, evidence-backed representation of:

- Where a device is expected to be observed.
- Which Readers actually observe it.
- How much power it contributes to each Reader.
- How confident the system is in that relationship.
- Which events support the relationship.
- Whether its contribution is individually identifiable.
- Whether the relationship is changing.
- Which topology and model versions were used.

The user-facing Matrix should be organized as:

**Devices as rows, Measurement Endpoints as hierarchical columns.**

## 9.1 Column Architecture — Multi-Level Headers

The columns must have multiple levels.

Recommended hierarchy:

```text
Electrical Domain
  └── Panel
       ├── Main Measurement Scope
       │     └── Main CT Reader
       │
       ├── Subpanel A
       │     ├── Subpanel A Reader
       │     ├── Circuit 12
       │     │     └── Circuit 12 CT Reader
       │     └── Circuit 14
       │           └── Circuit 14 CT Reader
       │
       └── Subpanel B
             ├── Subpanel B Reader
             └── Circuit 7
                   └── Circuit 7 CT Reader
```

A Smart Plug may be placed within a known circuit grouping when its installation location is known.

If its physical circuit is unknown, place it in an appropriate unassigned measurement group rather than inventing a location.

### Header levels

The UI should support these conceptual header levels:

| Level | Purpose |
|---|---|
| L0 | Electrical measurement domain |
| L1 | Main Panel or other electrical source |
| L2 | Subpanel or major measurement region |
| L3 | Circuit, breaker, or branch |
| L4 | Reader / Measurement Endpoint |

For example:

```text
                    Main Electrical Domain
                              |
                 +------------+-------------+
                 |                          |
            Main Panel                 Direct Devices
                 |                          |
        +--------+--------+               Plug P1
        |                 |
    Subpanel A        Subpanel B
        |                 |
     Circuit 12         Circuit 7
        |                 |
      CT-A12             CT-B07
```

The UI may render grouped column headers with `colSpan`.

Each Reader should appear as one actual leaf column.

Intermediate groups are organizational headers, not additional measurements.

**Do not create duplicate numerical columns for the same Reader at every level of the hierarchy.**

**Do not automatically sum parent and child Reader columns.**

The headers communicate measurement organization; they do not imply additive accounting.

## 9.2 Supporting Non-Tree Measurement Relationships

Real Measurement Topology may be a DAG or a graph rather than a perfect tree.

A Reader may participate in multiple relationships.

A device may be observed by multiple measurement domains.

Paired legs may represent the same physical device through separate electrical measurements.

Therefore:

- Each Reader must have one stable column identity.
- A primary display hierarchy may organize its column.
- Additional relationships must be displayed as relationship annotations or graph overlays.
- Secondary topology edges must not result in duplicate columns.
- A Reader must not be duplicated merely to make the UI tree look simpler.

The data model must not depend on the UI's column arrangement.

## 9.3 Row Architecture — Device-Centric

Rows should represent Physical Devices and their Load Components.

A typical row hierarchy:

```text
Living Room
  ├── Television
  │     ├── Display
  │     └── Standby Electronics
  │
  ├── Sound System
  └── Lighting
        ├── Main Lights
        └── Accent Lights

Kitchen
  ├── Refrigerator
  │     ├── Compressor
  │     └── Defrost Heater
  │
  └── Dishwasher

Unknown Loads
  ├── Unknown Load Profile 001
  └── Unknown Load Profile 002
```

The row hierarchy can represent rooms, device groups, or parent devices.

It must not imply that all child rows are independently identifiable.

A parent Physical Device row may summarize its Load Components.

Avoid adding parent and child estimates together.

## 9.4 Special Rows

The Matrix must support several types of rows.

### A. Physical Device Rows

Known devices.

### B. Load Component Rows

Individually modeled parts of a device.

### C. Device Group Rows

Groups of loads whose aggregate power is identifiable but whose individual contributions are not.

### D. Unknown Load Rows

Persistent unknown load profiles discovered from measurement evidence.

### E. Reader Residual Rows

Reader-specific unexplained consumption.

This is a measurement-scope residual, not automatically a physical device.

It must be clearly distinguished from Unknown Load Profiles.

A residual row must not be counted as an additional known device.

### F. System Summary Rows

Optional derived summaries, such as a device group's aggregate estimated consumption.

These must be clearly marked as derived values.

## 9.5 Matrix Example

The following is an illustrative two-level column structure.

Actual Reader identifiers should be displayed in the leaf headers.

| Device / Component | Main Panel / CT-MAIN | Subpanel A / CT-SA | Circuit 12 / CT-A12 | Device Endpoint / PLUG-TV |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Television | 88W | 88W | 88W | 88W |
| Sound System | 42W | 42W | 42W | — |
| Router | 12W | 12W | — | 12W |
| Refrigerator Compressor | 95W | — | — | — |
| Unknown Load 001 | 65W | 65W | 65W | — |
| Residual @ Circuit 12 | — | — | 18W | — |

This table is illustrative of measurement visibility.

The same 88W television contribution appearing in Main Panel, Subpanel, Circuit, and Plug columns is the same physical consumption observed at multiple measurement points.

It is not 352W of physical consumption.

A column represents what a measurement endpoint observes.

A row represents the physical load being explained.

The Matrix must preserve that distinction.

The actual cell values must also carry measurement/inference status. A Reader without sufficient evidence must not display a fabricated value merely to fill the table.

## 9.6 Matrix Cells Are Structured Objects

A Matrix cell must not be a single numeric watt value.

Each cell represents a relationship between one load and one Reader.

Use a structured model conceptually similar to:

```text
DeviceReaderCell
  device_id
  load_component_id
  reader_id

  mapping:
    observability
    relation_type
    mapping_status
    mapping_confidence
    valid_from
    valid_until

  power:
    measurement_status
    estimated_power_w
    min_power_w
    max_power_w
    standby_power_w
    operating_state
    expected_power_profile
    uncertainty

  event_association:
    matched_event_count
    confirmed_match_count
    provisional_match_count
    average_response_delay_ms
    response_signature
    last_matched_at

  confidence:
    measurement_quality
    event_association
    model_fit
    topology_consistency
    identifiability
    sample_coverage
    freshness

  provenance:
    sources_used
    evidence_refs
    topology_version
    model_version
    mapping_version

  timestamps:
    first_observed_at
    last_observed_at
    last_validated_at
    updated_at
```

The actual schema may use normalized relational tables, typed objects, or another appropriate structure.

Do not blindly store this entire nested object for every sample.

The Matrix is a materialized inference view derived from the underlying evidence and versioned models.

## 9.7 Mapping and Contribution Are Different

This distinction is mandatory.

A device may be visible to a Reader even when its current contribution is zero.

For example:

A television is physically connected to Circuit 12.

It is currently in standby.

The circuit Reader can observe it, even though its active contribution is small.

Therefore:

`observability` must be separate from `estimated_power_w`.

Recommended observability states:

- `DIRECT`
- `EXPECTED`
- `INFERRED`
- `PARTIAL`
- `UNKNOWN`
- `NOT_OBSERVABLE`
- `CONFLICTED`

Recommended mapping statuses:

- `UNKNOWN`
- `LEARNING`
- `PROVISIONAL`
- `CONFIRMED`
- `SUSPECTED_CHANGE`
- `CONFLICTED`
- `RETIRED`

The power value and its attribution status must be represented separately.

## 9.8 Mapping Matrix Is Sparse

Do not physically create every possible Device × Reader combination if most relationships are unknown.

For example, 500 devices and 100 Readers create 50,000 possible combinations.

Many combinations have no useful relationship.

Use a sparse logical Matrix.

Persist meaningful cells:

- Direct measurements.
- Candidate mappings.
- Validated mappings.
- Relevant negative observations.
- Conflicted mappings.
- Historical mappings.

Unknown combinations may be materialized as empty or `NOT_OBSERVABLE` cells by the query layer.

This reduces storage and prevents unnecessary updates.

## 9.9 Matrix Is Time-Dependent

The Matrix should have:

1. Current materialized view.
2. Versioned mapping history.
3. Immutable evidence references.

Do not store a complete Matrix snapshot for every Reader sample.

Update affected cells when:

- New measurement evidence changes the estimated contribution.
- A device model changes.
- A mapping gains evidence.
- A topology version changes.
- A new load component is identified.
- An unknown profile is merged or split.
- A device becomes non-identifiable.

The Matrix should support historical queries using the relevant version and evidence time.

## 9.10 Matrix Column and Row Aggregation Rules

Aggregation must be explicit.

For a Reader column:

\[
Y_r\approx
\sum_i H_{ri}P_i+U_r+\epsilon_r
\]

The Reader's unexplained residual is calculated relative to the valid measurement scope and compatible child evidence.

For physical total consumption, count each physical load once.

Never sum every Reader column to obtain total household consumption.

For a device parent row, do not add the parent estimate and its child component estimates if the parent is already defined as their aggregate.

The UI must distinguish:

- Measurement aggregation.
- Physical device aggregation.
- Reader-scope residual.
- Group-level inference.

These are different calculations.

# 10. Dynamic Matrix Matching and Updating

The Matrix should be updated continuously as the system learns.

It must not be treated as a permanent manual configuration table.

## 10.1 Initial Mapping

At startup, mapping knowledge may come from:

- Direct Smart Plug measurements.
- User-provided installation metadata.
- Known CT Reader locations.
- Existing topology.
- HA integrations.
- Historical event evidence.

Create initial mapping cells with an appropriate provisional status.

Do not assign high confidence merely because a device is configured in HA.

A configured HA device may have no currently validated electrical measurement relationship.

## 10.2 Event-Driven Mapping Updates

When a new Event Group is processed:

1. Identify affected Readers.
2. Find candidate devices.
3. Retrieve the current relevant Matrix cells.
4. Calculate predicted device-to-Reader signatures.
5. Match observed event signatures.
6. Run joint inference.
7. Assess identifiability.
8. Update evidence counters.
9. Update provisional contribution estimates.
10. Update confidence components.
11. Persist the mapping update.
12. Keep the previous mapping version available.

Only affected Matrix cells should be recalculated.

Do not recompute every cell on every event.

## 10.3 Confidence Evolution

Mapping confidence should accumulate through independent evidence.

Useful evidence includes:

- Repeated device transitions.
- Repeated Reader response signatures.
- Direct measurement.
- Consistent power amplitudes.
- Correct delayed response.
- Topology consistency.
- Independent events.
- Absence of contradictory evidence.

The system must recognize that 100 highly correlated samples from one event are not equivalent to 100 independent events.

Confidence should be evidence-aware.

A strong model fit does not guarantee identifiability.

A direct Smart Plug measurement may strongly establish the device's power even when its relationship to the larger circuit topology remains uncertain.

## 10.4 Matrix Reconciliation

Run periodic reconciliation to:

- Re-evaluate provisional mappings.
- Incorporate new topology versions.
- Update device power models.
- Retire invalid historical relationships.
- Detect contradictory evidence.
- Recalculate identifiability.
- Update unknown load profiles.

The reconciliation process must be deterministic and auditable.

# 11. Power Models

Every Physical Device or Load Component should have an appropriate power model.

## 11.1 Fixed Power

Examples:

- Simple lighting.
- Basic relay-controlled loads.
- Devices with stable ON and OFF behavior.

Estimate:

- Standby power.
- Active power.
- Transition delta.
- Variability.

Use robust statistics such as median and MAD where appropriate.

## 11.2 Adjustable Power

Examples:

- Dimmable lights.
- Variable-speed fans.
- Variable heating elements.

Model:

\[
P=f(x)
\]

where \(x\) represents the relevant HA attribute or control level.

Do not assume:

\[
P=kx
\]

unless actual evidence validates linearity.

Use piecewise models, lookup curves, monotonic interpolation, or another appropriate representation.

## 11.3 Dynamic Power

Examples:

- Refrigerators.
- HVAC systems.
- Washing machines.
- Dishwashers.

The same HA state may correspond to multiple physical operating states.

Use operating-state estimation, clustering, or state-machine models.

Do not assign one fixed power value to an entire appliance operating cycle.

## 11.4 Delayed and Transient Power

Examples:

- Blinds.
- Garage doors.
- HVAC startup.
- Motor-driven equipment.

Model temporal behavior.

Support:

- Startup.
- Active operation.
- Steady operation.
- Shutdown.
- Standby.

Where useful, model a response kernel conditioned on command direction or transition type.

## 11.5 Always-On and Standby Power

Many devices consume power when not actively operating.

At minimum distinguish:

- Standby.
- Idle.
- Active.
- Transient.
- Unknown state.

Use:

\[
\Delta P=P_{\text{new}}-P_{\text{old}}
\]

An OFF or standby state must not automatically imply 0W.

# 12. Real-Time Inference Output

The system should maintain two separate types of output.

## 12.1 Current Inference Snapshot

Represents the best current interpretation of the latest available measurements.

It should include:

```text
InferenceSnapshot
  snapshot_id
  observed_at
  computed_at

  topology_version
  model_version
  mapping_version

  reader_measurements[]
  device_estimates[]
  group_estimates[]
  residual_estimates[]

  system_quality
  stale_sources[]
  unresolved_events[]
```

## 12.2 Event Inference Record

Represents one particular Event Group and its causal interpretation.

Include:

- Event Group ID.
- Observed Reader deltas.
- Candidate devices.
- Chosen hypotheses.
- Rejected hypotheses.
- Unknown-load contribution.
- Estimated and bounded device changes.
- Identifiability.
- Confidence.
- Sources used.
- Model versions.
- Topology version.
- Evidence references.
- Final event status.

The snapshot may change as more evidence arrives.

The event evidence must remain immutable.

The event's inferred interpretation may be versioned and revised without altering the original measurements.

## 12.3 Provisional Versus Confirmed Estimates

Real-time results are not always final.

A useful lifecycle is:

1. `DETECTED`
2. `MATCHING`
3. `PROVISIONAL`
4. `CONFIRMED`
5. `REVISED`
6. `UNRESOLVED`

The system should be able to publish a provisional power estimate quickly, then revise it when delayed Readers or device responses arrive.

Do not block all user-facing inference until every device response window has completed.

Conversely, do not represent an early provisional value as final.

---

# 13. Learning and Feedback Control

Inference and learning must be separate processes.

A single event should not automatically rewrite a device's long-term power model.

## 13.1 Evidence Quality Gate

Before a measurement contributes to long-term learning, check:

- Reader measurement quality.
- Event association quality.
- Topology validity.
- Model consistency.
- Identifiability.
- Event independence.
- Residual ambiguity.

Only sufficiently reliable observations should update a confirmed model.

Provisional evidence may be retained for later reconciliation.

## 13.2 Incremental Learning

Use appropriate incremental methods for stable device models.

Examples:

- Running robust statistics for fixed-power loads.
- Online regression for adjustable loads.
- State-conditioned distributions for dynamic loads.
- Response-profile estimation for delayed loads.

Do not force every model class into one regression algorithm.

## 13.3 Historical Reconciliation

Periodically replay immutable evidence using the latest models and topology versions.

Recalculate derived outputs.

Do not rewrite original measurements.

Maintain old and new model versions and the evidence used to create them.

## 13.4 Active Learning

The system should eventually be able to identify which user action would provide the most useful new evidence.

For example:

If two lights always change together, suggest testing them independently.

Prioritize opportunities using:

- Current uncertainty.
- Expected measurement impact.
- Expected identifiability improvement.
- User effort.
- Operational safety.

This should be an optional user-guided improvement, not an excuse to automatically operate household devices without authorization.

---

# 14. Topology Change and Self-Healing

The Matrix and inference engines must support changing measurement configurations.

Examples:

- Smart Plug moved from Circuit A to Circuit B.
- CT Reader moved to another breaker.
- Reader replaced.
- CT orientation reversed.
- Calibration changed.
- Reader added or removed.
- Phase/leg assignment changed.

A topology change must not automatically erase a previously learned device power model.

Instead:

1. Record a Configuration Event.
2. Identify affected measurement relationships.
3. Mark affected mappings as `SUSPECTED_CHANGE`.
4. Reduce downstream confidence where necessary.
5. Generate candidate updated topology.
6. Collect new evidence.
7. Validate the candidate.
8. Promote a new topology version.
9. Reconcile the affected Matrix cells.
10. Preserve prior versions.

Mapping and power model versions must remain independent.

A Smart Plug can retain a valid device power model even when its circuit relationship changes.

---

# 15. Storage and Performance Behavior

The real-time architecture must not create excessive storage writes.

Use:

- High-resolution short-term buffers.
- Immutable Event Evidence.
- Aggregated long-term measurements.
- Versioned models and topology.
- Sparse Matrix persistence.
- Batch writes.
- Event-driven updates.

The Matrix should only be materialized when a relevant inference or mapping changes.

Do not create a full Matrix snapshot every second.

Store full raw event windows only when they provide meaningful evidence, with a defined retention policy.

Ensure the persisted event evidence remains sufficient to replay inference after detailed raw samples have expired.

The inference system should support restart and recovery without depending on hidden in-memory state.

---

# 16. Breaking Changes and Migration Policy

The mathematical and semantic correctness of the new architecture takes priority over preserving an incorrect legacy design.

Breaking changes are explicitly permitted.

Do not preserve an API or database schema merely because it has already been implemented.

However, breaking changes must be controlled.

Required practices:

1. Identify incompatible legacy fields and concepts.
2. Document why each change is necessary.
3. Introduce explicit schema and model versions.
4. Preserve immutable source measurements and historical evidence.
5. Create database migrations where applicable.
6. Mark legacy records whose measurement semantics are unknown.
7. Do not silently reinterpret old numbers as validated device power.
8. Provide migration and rollback procedures.
9. Update all dependent APIs and UI components together.
10. Update tests and requirement traceability.

The new canonical data model should prioritize:

- Physical Device.
- Load Component.
- HA Entity.
- Measurement Endpoint.
- Measurement Topology.
- Residual Model.
- Device Power Model.
- Event Group.
- Mapping Cell.
- Inference Snapshot.
- Evidence Reference.

If the existing implementation conflicts with these concepts, propose a clean replacement of the relevant model rather than accumulating compatibility layers indefinitely.

# 17. Mandatory End-to-End Algorithm Example

All Builders must understand and implement a unified example.

Assume:

```text
Main CT: 1,500W
  |
  +-- Subpanel CT: 800W
  |      |
  |      +-- Circuit A CT: 300W
  |      +-- Circuit B CT: 200W
  |
  +-- Circuit C CT: 400W
```

The Main Reader measures additional loads not represented by these child Readers.

A Smart Plug directly monitors the television.

The television has standby consumption.

A refrigerator is not connected to HA.

Two living room lights are connected to HA.

## Step 1 — Establish the current measurement state

The system normalizes all available measurements.

It validates their timing, measurement quality, and electrical domains.

## Step 2 — Maintain topology

The system uses existing validated measurement relationships.

Unexplained consumption remains in residual models.

## Step 3 — Detect a load event

The living room lights turn on.

HA reports the state changes.

Circuit A detects +45W.

Subpanel A detects +45W.

Main CT detects +45W.

## Step 4 — Dynamic matching

Both lights become candidates.

The topology indicates that the same physical load may appear in Circuit A, Subpanel A, and Main CT.

The system does not treat the three +45W observations as separate loads.

## Step 5 — Joint inference

If historical evidence supports individual light power models, estimate their contributions.

If they always switch together and cannot be separated, represent their combined group power without fabricating individual values.

## Step 6 — Matrix update

Update only the relevant cells:

- Light 1 × Main CT.
- Light 1 × Subpanel A.
- Light 1 × Circuit A.
- Light 2 × Main CT.
- Light 2 × Subpanel A.
- Light 2 × Circuit A.
- The relevant group-level cell if individual attribution is ambiguous.

The same physical contribution may appear in several Reader columns.

It is not counted several times.

## Step 7 — Unknown refrigerator activity

The refrigerator compressor starts without a HA event.

Circuit or panel measurements detect a load transition.

If an applicable Reader signature and known operating profile exist, the system can provisionally associate the event with the refrigerator.

Otherwise, it creates or updates an Unknown Load Profile.

The absence of HA evidence must not prevent detection.

## Step 8 — Smart Plug standby

The television remains in standby.

Its Smart Plug reports 3W.

The device's standby power model remains active.

The system must not report the device as consuming zero power merely because it is not actively operating.

## Step 9 — User moves the Smart Plug

The television Plug is moved to another circuit.

The system detects or receives a Configuration Event.

The old mapping becomes suspected or retired.

A new mapping candidate is created.

The television's own power model remains valid unless evidence suggests that its behavior has changed.

## Step 10 — Reconciliation

The system creates a new topology version, updates affected Matrix cells, preserves historical event evidence, and re-evaluates current estimates.

This workflow must function without manually rebuilding the entire system.

# 22. Final Architectural Rules

These rules override convenience-driven implementation decisions.

1. Reader measurements are evidence, not device identities.
2. Measurement Topology is inferred and versioned.
3. Physical Device, Load Component, HA Entity, and Measurement Endpoint are separate entities.
4. Parent residual is expected in partially observed systems.
5. Residual, unknown load, and measurement noise are distinct concepts.
6. Standby and idle power must be modeled.
7. Delta measurements and absolute measurements serve different purposes.
8. Multiple Reader observations of one device are not multiple physical loads.
9. Dynamic matching must be many-to-many and event-driven.
10. Joint inference must evaluate simultaneous candidate causes together.
11. An unknown-load alternative must always remain available.
12. Identifiability must be explicitly tested.
13. A mathematically ambiguous result must remain ambiguous.
14. Individual allocations must not be fabricated from group-level evidence.
15. Matrix cells represent structured device-to-reader relationships, not just watts.
16. Matrix columns are multi-level and Reader-based.
17. Matrix rows are device and load-component based, with special group and residual rows.
18. The Matrix is sparse, derived, versioned, and evidence-backed.
19. Matrix updates are event-driven, not performed for every measurement sample.
20. Topology, mapping, and device power models have independent version lifecycles.
21. Real-time inference and long-term model learning are separate processes.
22. Provisional inference can be revised without modifying immutable evidence.
23. Breaking changes are permitted when they improve mathematical correctness.
24. Historical evidence and explicit migration paths must be preserved.
25. The final UI must expose the real behavior and uncertainty of the algorithms.

**The system must optimize for correct explanations of electrical measurements, not for producing a device-level number for every measurement.**

A reliable 250W group estimate with an explicit non-identifiable status is better than two invented 125W device estimates.

A 200W unexplained residual that is preserved and tracked is better than falsely assigning that consumption to a known appliance.

A topology relationship marked uncertain is better than a confidently incorrect circuit hierarchy.

The central objective is to build a system that becomes more accurate as independent evidence accumulates, while remaining honest about what it cannot yet observe or identify.
